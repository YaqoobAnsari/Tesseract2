\section*{Reviewer 1}

We thank the reviewer for the thorough reading and for the assessment that the paper is well written and addresses a relevant problem with practical applications. We address each point below.

\commentone{R1.1}{Most importantly maybe, what exactly are your targeted applications? For example, why would you need / want corridors to be densely covered with nodes (and edges), at least in the original graph? For human navigation, for example, it appears sufficient to have a node at every `entry' to the corridor. And what is different in / with corridors than rooms that rooms do not receive the same treatment?}
\resp We agree this deserved an explicit answer, and we now give two: a stated application scope and a measured density trade-off. First, the revision adds a downstream-task validation section: accessibility (step-free) routing, which requires typed transitions, and per-room egress-distance analysis. Second, we ran a corridor-density ablation (grid spacings 20--80\,px, full pipeline reruns): at 60\,px spacing the graph remains fully connected with room-to-exit routes within 4.6\% of the 20\,px default at 57\% fewer nodes, while at 80\,px the corridor mesh fragments catastrophically (pairwise room completeness drops to 0.49). Density is therefore not required for topological reachability, but the sparse-failure mode is a cliff rather than a slope, and the default buys a safety margin against narrow corridors; we now state this with the measured curve. Rooms and corridors do receive analogous treatment (both are covered by spatial subnodes); the difference is the seeding --- rooms are seeded from their labels and sampled on a hexagonal grid within the flood-filled region, while corridors, being elongated unlabeled regions, are sampled on a uniform grid over the corridor mask. This distinction is now stated explicitly.
\loc{New downstream-validation subsection and density ablation in Sec.~10 [p.~XX]; corridor-vs-room sampling rationale in Sec.~6 [p.~XX]. Coloured blue.}

\commentone{R1.2}{Why do you perform spatial segmentation on a pixel level? Because you (still) operate on the image here? In principle, the extracted walls could provide boundaries of `closed' spaces with everything inside being part of that space, without explicitly assigning a `room' value to every pixel. That is, in other words, what is the underlying representation here and for subsequent steps? Is it the image space with pixels as the smallest unit?}
\resp Correct: the underlying representation throughout the pipeline is raster image space with pixels as the atomic unit, and we now say so explicitly in the problem-setting section. We chose pixel-level flood fill over wall-bounded polygonization because (a) legacy drawings frequently contain hairline gaps, decorative strokes, and symbol overlaps that make robust closed-polygon extraction brittle, while flood fill degrades gracefully, and (b) the pixel sets directly feed the distance-transform subnode placement. Vectorized boundaries are attractive future work and are now mentioned as such.
\loc{Representation statement in Sec.~3.1; design rationale in Sec.~6.2 [p.~XX]. Coloured blue.}

\commentone{R1.3}{Why is it reasonable to use the position of the text label for a room as the position for the main node? The label could be arbitrarily placed in principle. What makes that location so special?}
\resp The reviewer is right that nothing \emph{forces} labels to be central --- so we measured it. Across 172 rooms on nine processed floor sections, the offset between the label position and the geometric centroid of the room's flood-filled region has median 0.24 room-equivalent-radii (18\,px), with 92\% of labels falling inside the room's inscribed circle and a worst case of 0.98 radii. Architectural drafting practice places labels centrally, and the revision reports this measurement as the empirical justification. We have also removed the term ``semantic centroid'' (cf.\ R1.17) in favour of the precise description: the label position, empirically near-central, refined against the region's geometric centroid.
\loc{Measurement reported in Sec.~6.3 [p.~XX]; terminology corrected throughout. Coloured blue.}

\commentone{R1.4}{And then (page 9, `room family funneling') you state that you root all `subnode-to-door' paths through the main node. I do fail to see how Algorithm 1 actually guarantees this. You seem to compute all shortest paths from any node in the room to the one closest to the door. But these paths do not necessarily contain the main node. All you do (as far as I can tell) is add an edge from the main node to the door as well. You may well end up with `subnode-to-door' paths that do not go through the main node. Or am I missing something? Along the same lines: why would you want all paths in a room to go through the main node?}
\resp The reviewer is not missing anything --- this observation is correct, and we thank them for it. The implementation deliberately retains both the closest-family-node--to--door edge and the main-node--to--door edge, so subnode-to-door paths near a door legitimately bypass the main node (on our primary evaluation floor, 69 of 186 such shortest paths do). The conference phrasing overclaimed. In the revision we state and prove the property that actually holds and that downstream routing actually needs: \emph{complete} intra-room connectivity (every family node reaches every room door) at \emph{linear} intra-room edge cost --- the retained edges form a spanning tree of the family (at most $|S|$ edges for $|S|$ subnodes, versus $\Theta(|S|\cdot|D_r|)$ for dense wiring), plus one closest-node edge per door. Algorithm~1 is rewritten to describe the real construction (transient proximity lattice, anchor-rooted shortest-path tree, shortcut edge), a Proposition with proof replaces the informal guarantee, and a three-way ablation (dense wiring / funneling / one-node-per-room) shows funneling achieves completeness with 167 intra-room edges versus 1,869 dense at only 7.2\% route stretch. To the reviewer's final question: we do \emph{not} want all paths through the main node --- the shortcut edge strictly improves realized path lengths --- and the revised text says so explicitly.
\loc{Rewritten Sec.~7.3: Algorithm~1, Proposition~1 with proof, honest-correction paragraph, complexity analysis; ablation in Sec.~10 [p.~XX]. Coloured blue (raised in spirit also by R2's ablation request; joint passages vermilion).}

As it stands, it is really hard to tell how `easy' or `complex' the task is [...] I'd be perfectly fine with having these figures in an appendix if they take up too much space in the main text.}

\commentone{R1.6}{More importantly, having just a single example / set of floor plans makes for a somewhat poor evaluation. [...] I'd strongly encourage you to include two or three additional buildings in the evaluation, ideally not from the same agency [...] By the way, the SIGSpatial paper seems to include three sites. Why are they not all included in this paper any longer?}

\loc{Sec.~10.1 dataset table and multi-site results in Sec.~10 [p.~XX]. Coloured blue (also R2-W3/R3-1c; joint passages vermilion).}

\commentone{R1.7}{I understand the aim to maintain `true distances.' However, even on pixel-level precision, the navigable shortest path ($d_{graph}$) between two rooms will in almost all circumstances be (substantially) longer than the direct line distance between the two centroids [...] Why is this a meaningful metric then? However, later on in Section 10.7 you claim that $d_{graph}$ is very close to $d_{euclidean}$. [...] Please clarify!}
\resp The reviewer's analysis is correct, and rather than clarifying the Euclidean proxy we replaced it with an objective reference: the \emph{free-space geodesic} --- the true shortest walkable path through the drawing's non-wall pixels (doorways opened at detected door positions), computed exactly on the raster. Against this reference, graph routes on the primary (enclosed) evaluation floor are within 4.7\% of optimal on average (p90: 9.3\%). We report the reference in two variants that bracket the truth (a permissive full-free-space bound and a conservative interior-only bound excluding semantically outdoor space), and we additionally report the ratio geodesic/Euclidean --- which runs 1.35--1.67 on average and up to $3.3\times$ --- as the quantified reason the straight-line proxy was misleading, exactly as the reviewer argued. Rendered overlays (geodesic vs.\ graph route on the floorplan) accompany the revision for visual verification.
\loc{Sec.~10 fidelity subsection rewritten around the geodesic reference [p.~XX]. Coloured blue (also R2-W7, R3-5; joint passages vermilion).}

\commentone{R1.8}{You state that the major issues in the workflow arise from failed door detection. I can perfectly accept this. However, I'd still be interested to learn how good the text label detection works? Do you manage to extract all labels? 95\%? 80\%?... What happens if there are labels that are not identified (at all)?}
\resp We now measure it: against a hand-enumerated ground truth of every label visible on the primary evaluation drawing, detection is 43/43 (100\%) and recognition 41/43 (95.3\%); both failures truncate a leading digit of a room number (\eg, ``1011''$\rightarrow$``101''). The failure consequences differ by class and are now stated: a \emph{misrecognized numeric} label still yields a valid room node (only the name is wrong; connectivity is unaffected), whereas a label missed \emph{entirely} leaves its region without a semantic seed --- the region is not instantiated, which we demonstrate with an unlabeled variant of the same drawing that yields zero transition nodes.
\loc{Text-accuracy paragraph in Sec.~10.2 with failure-mode analysis [p.~XX]. Coloured blue.}

\commentone{R1.9}{Finally, the only actual extension compared to the original paper seems to be the ability to combine several floor plans to a multi-level graph [...] the way this step is realized, it appears to be almost trivial from a scientific or algorithmic perspective [...] all that is left to do then is to add indices to all labels, and to perform some simple assertions. [...] to me makes for a very weak extension of the existing paper when it comes to judging the `delta' between the two manuscripts.}
\resp This criticism was fair for the submitted version, and it motivated the largest addition of the revision: the correspondence problem is now solved \emph{automatically}. The revised system infers which stairs/elevators on adjacent floors are the same physical shaft by (i) registering the two drawings into a common frame --- candidate similarity transforms from shaft pairs, phase correlation of wall masks, and a drawing-scale prior bootstrapped from detected door widths, ranked by a symmetric chamfer distance between glyph-filtered wall masks and refined by ICP --- and (ii) solving a global optimal assignment \emph{with a rejection option}, so shafts that terminate remain unmatched rather than being force-paired. The manual mapping file is demoted to an optional override, and the six validation checks now verify the machine's answer rather than a human's input. Robustness is evaluated systematically: under known synthetic perturbations of a real floor, matching accuracy is 1.000 through 400\,px translation, 30$^\circ$ rotation, 1.4$\times$ scale, twin-shaft separations down to 25\,px, and shaft-deletion tests, while a nearest-neighbour baseline degrades to 0.75 on close twins; registration recovery error is 0.5--4.9\,px median. [Real multi-floor precision/recall against the hand-mapping ground truth to be added with the extended dataset.] We believe this changes the delta assessment: the extension now contains a non-trivial algorithmic contribution (joint registration and assignment with rejection) rather than bookkeeping plus assertions.
\loc{Sec.~9 substantially rewritten: new automatic-matching subsections + robustness evaluation in Sec.~10 [p.~XX]. Coloured vermilion (raised jointly by R1, R2-W1, R3-2).}

\commenttwo{R2.W3}{Door detection remains the dominant source of graph failure. In particular, corridor-to-corridor doors are much less reliable than room-to-corridor and exit doors. Since missed corridor doors can fragment the graph, this limitation deserves stronger treatment and possibly additional technical improvement.}
\resp We strengthened the treatment with a systematic degradation study that also sharpens the claim. Monte-Carlo door-knockout experiments (deleting 0--50\% of detected doors, 20 draws per level, by type) show that \emph{most} corridor-to-corridor doors are redundant through the corridor mesh --- but the sole-bridge ones are individually catastrophic: deleting one single corridor door on one evaluation floor costs 0.51 of pairwise room completeness and 0.54 of exit reachability by itself, while room-to-corridor deletions cause bounded, linear damage (each severs one room). Crucially, bridge doors are identifiable in-graph as cut vertices, which yields a principled verification/repair target; the revision reports the curves, the single-door sensitivities, and this targeting observation, and outlines graph-guided repair as the concrete next mechanism.
\loc{New degradation subsection in Sec.~10 + discussion [p.~XX]. Coloured green (curves shared with R3-3, vermilion).}

\commenttwo{R2.W4}{The paper should clarify whether doors are modeled as undirected or directed transitions, especially for exit doors, emergency exits, or one-way access constraints. The meaning of ``exit door'' should also be defined more precisely.}
\resp Clarified: the graph is undirected; an exit door is defined as a door whose two incident regions are an interior space and an outdoor region. One-way and emergency-egress constraints are not modeled and are now listed explicitly as a limitation with the natural extension (directional edge attributes).
\loc{Sec.~3.2 definition + Sec.~11 limitation [p.~XX]. Coloured green.}
